Ledgers, groups and vouchers:

When we enter a transaction in Tally, the first thing we need to do is create the accounts involved in that transaction, i.e., create their **Ledgers**.

When the same type of entry occurs again, we do not need to create the Ledger again because it has already been created.

In Tally, the **Cash (Cash-in-Hand)** and **Profit & Loss Account** Ledgers are already created by default.

When we create a Ledger for an account in Tally, we also have to assign a **Group** to it at the same time. The Ledger is placed under that particular Group.

To understand which Ledger should belong to which Group, we first need to understand the different Groups and the different types of Ledgers.

For example:

**Rahul paid ₹2,000 as office rent.**

Here, rent is a type of expense, so:

**Debit:** Rent
**Credit:** Cash

**Ledger:** Rent
**Under:** Indirect Expenses

If Rahul pays ₹2,000 as rent for the next month, we do not need to create the Rent Ledger again because it was already created in the previous month.

So the entry will simply be:

**Debit:** Rent
**Credit:** Cash

A **Voucher** is a type of document in which we record the transactions that we enter into Tally. Tally already has several types of vouchers. To decide which voucher should be used for a particular transaction, we need to understand the different types of vouchers.

Tally has predefined Groups. Every Ledger that we create in Tally is placed under a particular Group.

There are two types of Groups:

1. Primary Groups
2. Secondary Groups


1. PRIMARY GROUPS

1. Capital Account - Liability
2. Loans (Liability) - Liability
3. Current Liabilities - Liability
4. Fixed Assets - Asset
5. Investments - Asset
6. Current Assets - Asset
7. Miscellaneous Expenses (Assets) - Asset
8. Suspense Account - Liability
9. Branch/Divisions - Liability
10. Sales Account - Income
11. Purchase Account - Expenses
12. Direct Income - Income
13. Direct Expenses - Expenses
14. Indirect Income - Income
15. Indirect Expenses - Expenses


2. SECONDARY GROUPS

1. Reserves & Surplus - Capital Account
2. Bank OD (Bank Overdraft) - Loans (Liability)
3. Secured Loans - Loans (Liability)
4. Unsecured Loans - Loans (Liability)

WHY GROUPS ARE IMPORTANT

Groups tell Tally what type of account a Ledger represents.

Tally uses this classification to prepare financial reports such as:

- Balance Sheet
- Profit & Loss Account
- Trial Balance
- Other accounting reports

Therefore, while creating a Ledger, we must select the correct Group.

PREDEFINED GROUPS IN TALLY – DETAILED EXPLANATION

1. CAPITAL ACCOUNT

The money invested in a business to start or run the business is called Capital.

In Tally, when we create the Ledger for the owner's capital, we keep it under the Capital Account group.

Capital can be in different forms, such as cash, bank balance, assets introduced into the business, etc.


2. LOANS (LIABILITY)

When we take any type of loan for the business, the account is placed under Loans (Liability).

Examples:
- Business Loan
- Bank Loan
- Other Borrowings


3. CURRENT LIABILITIES

When a business has short-term liabilities or amounts that have to be paid within a short period, such accounts come under Current Liabilities.

Examples:
- Sundry Creditors
- Duties & Taxes
- Provisions


4. FIXED ASSETS

To run a business, we need to purchase various long-term assets such as:

- Computer
- Telephone
- Air Conditioner (A/C)
- Furniture
- Machinery

These assets are generally used for a long period and cannot be easily converted into cash through normal business transactions.

Such accounts are placed under Fixed Assets.


5. INVESTMENTS

When business money is invested somewhere with the purpose of earning a return or profit, the account is placed under Investments.

Examples:
- Long-term Investments
- Fixed Deposits (FD)
- Shares
- Mutual Funds


6. CURRENT ASSETS

Assets that can be easily converted into cash or used in the short term are called Current Assets.

Examples:
- Cash
- Bank Balance
- Sundry Debtors
- Short-term Advances
- Stock


7. MISCELLANEOUS EXPENSES (ASSETS)

These are certain expenses incurred mainly at the beginning of a business or for specific purposes, which are treated as assets in traditional accounting classification.

Examples:
- Preliminary Expenses
- Company Logo-related expenses
- Copyright-related expenses
- Trademark expenses
- Stamp Duty
- Legal Fees


8. SUSPENSE ACCOUNT

Sometimes an entry may be missing, incomplete, or the correct account may not be known at the time of recording the transaction.

In such cases, the amount may temporarily be placed in a Suspense Account until the correct account is identified.

This helps avoid incorrect financial reports while the transaction is being investigated or corrected.


9. BRANCH/DIVISIONS

When a company has different branches or divisions, the accounts related to those branches or divisions can be maintained under the Branch/Divisions group.


10. SALES ACCOUNT

When a business sells goods or services, the sales account is placed under Sales Account.

Example:

Goods sold = Sales


11. PURCHASE ACCOUNT

When a business purchases goods for the purpose of selling them or for its business operations, the purchase account is placed under Purchase Account.

Example:

Goods purchased for resale = Purchase


12. DIRECT INCOME

Income that is directly related to the main business activity is called Direct Income.

It is income generated from the primary business operations.

Example:

For a manufacturing business, income directly related to its main business activity may be treated as Direct Income.


13. INDIRECT INCOME

Income that is not directly generated from the main business activity is called Indirect Income.

Examples:
- Discount Received
- Commission Received
- Interest Received


14. DIRECT EXPENSES

Direct Expenses are expenses directly related to the production or purchase of goods or services that the business sells.

Examples:
- Manufacturing Expenses
- Direct Labour
- Carriage Inward
- Transportation directly related to bringing goods into the business

These expenses are directly connected with the cost of goods or production.


15. INDIRECT EXPENSES

Indirect Expenses are expenses that are not directly related to the production or purchase of goods.

They are generally expenses incurred for running and managing the business.

Examples:
- Rent Expenses
- Salary Expenses
- Office Expenses
- Discount Allowed
- Electricity Expenses


16. RESERVES & SURPLUS

Amounts set aside from profits for future use or specific purposes are generally maintained under Reserves & Surplus.

These form part of the owner's/shareholders' funds or capital structure of the business.


17. BANK OVERDRAFT

When a bank allows a business to withdraw more money than the amount available in its bank account, it is called a Bank Overdraft (OD).

Example:

A business has ₹10,000 in its bank account, but the bank provides an OD limit that allows it to withdraw more than ₹10,000.

The overdraft amount is treated as a liability.


18. SECURED LOANS

Loans that are taken against security or collateral are called Secured Loans.

Examples:
- Home Loan
- Gold Loan
- Car Loan
- Loan against Property


19. UNSECURED LOANS

Loans that are taken without providing specific security or collateral are called Unsecured Loans.

Examples:
- Personal Loan
- Business Loan
- Certain Credit Facilities
- Credit Card Outstanding


20. DUTIES & TAXES

Accounts related to different taxes and statutory duties are placed under Duties & Taxes.

Examples:
- GST
- TDS
- VAT
- Other applicable taxes


21. PROVISIONS

A provision is an amount set aside to cover an expected expense or liability.

Examples:
- Provision for Salary
- Provision for Rent
- Provision for Other Expected Expenses


22. SUNDRY CREDITORS

When a business purchases goods or services on credit, the business owes money to the supplier.

Such supplier accounts are generally placed under Sundry Creditors.

Example:

ABC Traders supplies goods worth ₹50,000 on credit.

ABC Traders → Sundry Creditors


23. SUNDRY DEBTORS

When a business sells goods or services on credit, the customer owes money to the business.

Such customer accounts are generally placed under Sundry Debtors.

Example:

Rahul Traders purchases goods worth ₹30,000 on credit.

Rahul Traders → Sundry Debtors


24. DEPOSITS (ASSET)

When the business deposits money as security for something, the account is placed under Deposits (Asset).

Examples:
- Security Deposit for Rent
- Security Deposit for Electricity
- Other Refundable Deposits


25. LOANS & ADVANCES (ASSET)

When a business gives money to someone as a loan or advance, the amount is treated as an asset for the business.

Examples:
- Advance given to an employee
- Advance given to a supplier
- Loan given to another party


26. CASH-IN-HAND

This group is used for cash accounts.

It represents physical cash available with the business.

Example:

Cash in office = Cash-in-Hand


27. STOCK-IN-HAND

This group is used for stock or inventory held by the business.

Examples:
- Raw Material
- Finished Goods
- Trading Goods
- Other Inventory


28. BANK ACCOUNTS

This group is used for bank accounts maintained by the business.

Examples:
- SBI Current Account
- HDFC Bank Account
- ICICI Bank Account
- Other Business Bank Accounts

PREDEFINED VOUCHERS IN TALLY – DETAILED EXPLANATION


1. CONTRA VOUCHER

When we transfer money between Cash and Bank, we use the Contra Voucher.

Examples:
- Cash deposited into Bank
- Cash withdrawn from Bank
- Transfer between Bank Accounts
- Transfer between Cash and Bank

Example:

Cash deposited into Bank = ₹10,000

Debit: Bank A/c
Credit: Cash A/c

This entry is recorded using a Contra Voucher.


2. PAYMENT VOUCHER

When we make any type of payment, whether by Cash or Cheque, we record the transaction using a Payment Voucher.

Examples:
- Rent paid in cash
- Salary paid by cheque
- Electricity bill paid
- Payment made to a supplier

Example:

Rent paid by Cash = ₹5,000

Debit: Rent A/c
Credit: Cash A/c

Voucher: Payment Voucher


3. RECEIPT VOUCHER

When we receive money through Cash or Cheque, we record the transaction using a Receipt Voucher.

Examples:
- Cash received from a customer
- Cheque received from a customer
- Interest received
- Commission received

Example:

Cash received from Rahul = ₹10,000

Debit: Cash A/c
Credit: Rahul A/c

Voucher: Receipt Voucher


4. JOURNAL VOUCHER

Journal Voucher is used for various types of accounting entries that are not normally recorded through Cash or Bank transactions.

Examples:
- Adjustment Entries
- Provisions
- Depreciation
- Credit transactions in certain cases
- Other accounting adjustments

Journal Voucher is commonly used for entries where there is no immediate Cash or Bank movement.

Example:

Depreciation on Machinery = ₹5,000

Debit: Depreciation A/c
Credit: Machinery A/c

Voucher: Journal Voucher


5. PURCHASE VOUCHER

When a business purchases goods for resale or for business purposes, the transaction is recorded using a Purchase Voucher.

Example:

Goods purchased for ₹20,000

Debit: Purchase A/c
Credit: Supplier A/c

Voucher: Purchase Voucher


6. SALES VOUCHER

When a business sells goods or services, the transaction is recorded using a Sales Voucher.

Example:

Goods sold for ₹30,000

Debit: Customer A/c
Credit: Sales A/c

Voucher: Sales Voucher


7. DEBIT NOTE

A Debit Note is generally used when goods purchased from a supplier are returned.

This is called a Purchase Return.

Example:

Goods worth ₹5,000 returned to the supplier.

Debit Note is used to record the Purchase Return.

Example entry:

Debit: Supplier A/c
Credit: Purchase Return A/c


8. CREDIT NOTE

A Credit Note is generally used when goods sold to a customer are returned by the customer.

This is called a Sales Return.

Example:

Customer returns goods worth ₹3,000.

Credit Note is used to record the Sales Return.

Example entry:

Debit: Sales Return A/c
Credit: Customer A/c


9. STOCK JOURNAL

When goods or stock are transferred from one Godown to another Godown, we use the Stock Journal Voucher.

Example:

Goods transferred:

Godown A → Godown B

Stock Journal is used to record this transfer.

There is no sale or purchase involved; only the location of the stock changes.


10. MEMORANDUM VOUCHER

If we want to record an entry temporarily for future reference or remember a transaction without affecting the regular accounting books immediately, we can use a Memorandum Voucher.

A Memorandum Voucher is generally used for temporary or informational entries.

It does not immediately affect the regular accounting records in the same way as a normal accounting voucher.


11. PURCHASE ORDER

When a business wants to purchase goods from a supplier, it can send a list of required items and quantities to the supplier.

This is recorded using a Purchase Order.

Example:

Purchase Order:

- 100 units of Item A
- 50 units of Item B
- 20 units of Item C

The Purchase Order can also be printed and sent to the supplier.

Important:

A Purchase Order is an order to purchase. It is not the actual purchase transaction.


12. SALES ORDER

When a customer places an order to purchase goods from our business, we record that order using a Sales Order.

Example:

Customer orders:

- 100 units of Item A
- 50 units of Item B

The order is recorded using a Sales Order.

Important:

A Sales Order is an order received from the customer. It is not the actual sales transaction.


13. DELIVERY NOTE

After receiving a Sales Order, when the goods are delivered to the customer, we can use a Delivery Note.

Flow:

Customer places order
        ↓
Sales Order
        ↓
Goods are delivered
        ↓
Delivery Note


14. REVERSING JOURNAL

A Reversing Journal is used for certain temporary accounting entries where we want the effect of the entry to remain only for a particular date or period.

The entry is automatically reversed after the specified period/date.

It is useful for temporary adjustments and accounting purposes.


15. RECEIPT NOTE

After placing a Purchase Order with a supplier, when the supplier delivers the goods to us, we can record the receipt of those goods using a Receipt Note.

Flow:

Purchase Order
        ↓
Supplier sends goods
        ↓
Receipt Note
        ↓
Goods received


16. PHYSICAL STOCK

Physical Stock Voucher is used when we physically count the stock available in the Godown and compare it with the stock shown in Tally.

Example:

Tally Stock = 100 units
Physical Stock = 95 units

The Physical Stock Voucher can be used to record the actual physical quantity.

This helps identify differences between:

Book Stock
and
Physical Stock


17. PAYROLL

The Payroll Voucher is used for recording payroll-related transactions in Tally.

It can be used for employee salary and payroll processing.

Examples:
- Salary
- Employee earnings
- Deductions
- Other payroll-related calculations


18. ATTENDANCE

The Attendance Voucher is used to record employee attendance information for payroll purposes.

Examples:
- Present
- Absent
- Paid Leave
- Unpaid Leave
- Overtime

Attendance information can then be used in payroll calculations.
